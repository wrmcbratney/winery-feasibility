Assumptions

How the model works

Timeline. Year 0 is land, site work, engineering and the build (or planting). The first harvest is year 1. Each vintage is released after its months-to-release, counted from a mid-October harvest, and bottled about three months before release. Sales follow a brand ramp from first release (35, 55, 75, 90, then 100% of target), oldest vintage first; unsold wine waits in inventory.

Building. The winery is built up space by space: covered crush pad, fermentation hall sized to the tank count, barrel room sized to the barrel stock, case storage sized to the peak of bottled inventory (if kept on site), lab, tasting room, and about 12% for offices, restrooms and mechanical. Each style has its own cost per square foot for each space and its own design and engineering fee, which climbs from about 8% of construction for a metal building to 18% for a gravity-flow hillside design, plus a site work share that jumps when you cut into a slope. Gravity flow removes the must pump and most hose runs but costs far more in concrete and excavation. Buildings depreciate over 30 years.

Storage and tax. Washington exempts business inventory, including bottled wine, from property tax, so storing on site costs the extra building, plus property tax, insurance and upkeep on that building. Off-site bonded storage swaps that for per-case monthly fees, handling, and an admin fee for bonded tax reporting. Federal excise tax is due when wine leaves bond either way, so it is not part of this choice and is not modeled yet.

Lab. Analyses scale with lot count (tons per lot sets how many lots) times analyses per lot per year. Sending everything out costs the outside price per analysis. A basic in-house lab moves about 60% of routine work (pH, TA, SO2, Brix, VA) in-house at reagent cost plus lab time; a full lab with an enzymatic analyzer moves about 90%. Both still send out the rest.

Equipment sizing. Tanks and barrels come from the cellar flow: for each vessel type, the most vessels in use on any day of a normal year, plus a design margin for "not quite worst case." Ferment and white tanks hold one lot each; lot tanks can combine lots of the same wine after press. Each tank type is the smallest standard size that holds its largest load, priced on a size curve (bigger tanks cost less per gallon), and blending tanks hold a whole wine. Barrels are the peak in use plus spares, which also sets the first year's barrel purchase. Press and chiller follow from tonnage. Pumps, hoses and fittings follow from the tank count; pumpover pumps are one per four red tanks. Barrels follow months in barrel and new-oak share; year one buys the whole working stock.

Costs. Each vintage carries its fruit, estate farming, cellar labor and supplies (or custom crush fees), barrels and dry goods; that cost per case becomes cost of goods when it sells. Salaries, tasting room, utilities, insurance, maintenance, marketing and card fees are period costs. Depreciation: building 30 years, equipment 10 (half replaced in year 10), vineyard 25.

Financing and the owner. The loan covers a share of years 0 to 2 capital spending, amortized monthly-equivalent over its term. In both cases the owner injects equity whenever cash would drop below the reserve, and takes distributions once operations are cash-positive and cash exceeds twice the reserve. If the owner is the winemaker, the owner's salary counts as payout and as a cost; if a winemaker is hired, the owner is an investor only. The 20-year IRR includes an ending value: land at its growth rate, undepreciated assets, inventory at cost and cash, less debt.

Land. Vineyard acres follow from production: tons needed for each wine divided by its tons per acre. Only part of the vineyard ground can be planted (roads, headlands and turn rows, setbacks, rock and steep ground), so planted acres are grossed up by the plantable share. The winery site is sized from the building at a site-coverage ratio. The total is rounded up to the zoning minimum parcel, since rural agricultural zones in eastern Washington commonly set 20- or 40-acre minimums; any extra is spare land that holds value but earns nothing. Irrigated farmland is priced only when the vineyard waters from the land's own right; a winery on bought fruit, or a vineyard using a purchased right, buys cheaper dry ground, and raw ground pays to be cleared before planting.

Water. A winery's process water usually fits under Washington's permit-exempt well rules (up to 5,000 gallons a day for industrial use), so the winery gets its own well; the busiest harvest day is checked against that limit. A vineyard of any real size needs a water right. Three ways in: land inside an irrigation district (the right comes with the land; you pay an annual assessment), land with an existing groundwater right and well (you pay to pump), or cheaper dry land plus a water right bought from someone else, a new well, and an Ecology change application whose timing and approval aren't guaranteed. A bought water right is carried like land: it doesn't depreciate and it counts in the ending value.

Excise. Federal tax is $1.07 a gallon on still wine up to 16%, less the small-producer credit of $1.00 a gallon on the first 30,000 gallons removed from bond each year, so a winery this size pays about 7 cents a gallon. Washington's wine tax of $0.2292 a liter applies to wine the winery sells to Washington consumers; wine sold to distributors is taxed when they buy it. Washington taxes wine over 14% at the higher fortified rate unless it got there by natural fermentation and an affidavit is on file, so the model assumes the affidavit. Other states' taxes on out-of-state club shipments are not modeled.

Bottling. Bottling is the winemaker's call, set per wine in the table as months after harvest. The default is about a month after barrel time ends (reds reach barrel in spring, around month 6), with release at least two months after bottling. If bottling comes before the barrel time is up, the barrel time is cut short and the flow tab says so. Moving bottling changes when blending tanks, barrels and case storage free up, and which year the dry goods are bought.

Empty barrels. Barrels sit empty between the old vintage coming out and the new one going in after ML. The model counts empty barrel-days from the flow and adds sulfuring every six weeks to the labor, and the equipment list carries a barrel washing station, a hot-water pressure washer to drive it, a sulfur kit and, if chosen, an ozone generator. Racks cover every installed barrel, full or empty.

Cellar flow. Each wine is split into lots of the chosen size, picked one every two days from the variety's typical Washington start date. Reds run through the lot simulator for their own pick date: days in the ferment tank, press, then ML in a storage tank until the cellar warms in spring, then barrel for the months set, back to tank before bottling. Whites settle two days, ferment in tank or go to barrel, then wait in tank for bottling. Bottled wine waits for release and sells evenly over the following year. Several identical vintages are laid on top of each other, so a normal year shows older wine still in barrel and bottle while the new one comes in. Tank lines count tank space including headspace during fermentation.

Daily labor and equipment. The flow calendar also drives the work. Each lot adds its pick-day crew and sorting, press loads, twice-daily pumpovers while in tank, ferment checks, a transfer and tank cleaning at every move, barrel filling, monthly topping, a mid-term racking and washing on the way out, and bottling days on a mobile line; sold cases add shipping work. Harvest is any day a ferment is running. The crew is sized from these days: full-time staff from the off-season workload, seasonal workers from the busiest harvest week, so the dashed line shows where long days and catch-up fall. Equipment hours come from the same events, against one 10-hour shift per unit.

Labor. Cellar labor is built from tasks. Harvest work scales with pick days (one per lot), tons sorted, press loads, red ferment days (from the lot simulator: cold soak, ferment and any extended maceration) times pumpovers, and tank turnovers, plus daily rounds whether fruit arrives or not. The rest of the year scales with barrels, storage tanks and cases. The winemaker covers a set number of hands-on hours. A full-time cellar worker is hired once the leftover off-season work reaches about 60% of a full-time job; that person works long harvest weeks with overtime past 40 hours, and seasonal workers fill whatever harvest hours remain. Labor is a production cost, so it rides in each vintage's cost per case. Salaried winemaker, admin and tasting room staff stay separate.

Not yet. Production growing over time, income taxes, winery wastewater, risk (bad vintages, missed ramps), price inflation, and the quality value of a hired winemaker, which is why hiring currently looks like pure cost.
